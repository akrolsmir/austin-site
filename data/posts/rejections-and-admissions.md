Have you ever been part of an admissions committee? Set up your own admissions pipeline? Have you before been the arbiter of character, the judge of talent, your finger poised above a “yes” or a “no”?

I have, and, it sucks. Reviewing applications is corrosive to the soul. I’m asked to strip away someone’s essential humanity, reducing them down to a question of whether they are useful or valuable, whether they are fit for inclusion. I don’t understand how one is supposed to engage in this process without emerging, fundamentally altered, more instrumental and less compassionate.

It sure would be nice to never have to judge anyone again.

#### Judge not, lest ye be judged

I have often been judged, and often found wanting. These days it’s more silent, when I ping an acquaintance to fund some project of mine, and never hear back. It’s almost a mercy — I can then pretend that they were simply too busy, maybe never even laid eyes on my proposal. (Though I’d ultimately prefer the grace of an explicit “no”).

It wasn’t always this way, though. I was rejected from my first EAG. Rejected from the Future Forum, from EA Bahamas. Every single time I’ve applied to YCombinator. And countless, countless grant applications.

There’s this new website, [aisafety.fail](https://aisafety.fail/), which satirizes the silent rejections inherent in the space of AI safety. I don’t think AI safety does particularly badly — in fact, I expect its hiring norms to be exemplary compared to wider tech or nonprofits — but, I get it. One face of the AI safety shoggoth whispers, “apply, apply, we’re desperate for talent, you could save the world, just apply”. And you think, “wow, that could be me”, and you spend weeks of your life lurking and reading and build up the werewithal to apply, and then another shoggoth whacks you on the head, “didn’t you know? we wanted agency, we wanted talent. not, you.”

#### The parable of the talents

The parable of the talents haunts me. (Partly because I’m not sure which of the servants I am — have I been given five talents? two? one? How much of a return is expected of me?)

But mostly because it paints a picture of an unforgiving master, him who judges his servants based on their outcomes, and casts the unsuccessful ones into hell. Even if I can make my own quota, I’d like to think that my peers will be forgiven theirs. But this does not seem to be the moral of the story. Rather, the moral seems to be, “

I do have some sympathy for the plight of the master. In fact, I now sit atop some resources which are scarce. My orgs have a limited amount of money with which to hire people. My spaces and events can only fit so many folks. And my own time and focus becomes scarcer and scarcer. These days, every time I meet someone new, I suspect that another slips out of my context window.

So: maybe it sounds noble to never have to choose, never have to prioritize. But maybe this a dereliction of duty?

Certainly, people lend me their time, their money, their attention, on the idea that I make good judgments. And people appreciate being in curated environments, surrounded by likeminded, curious peers. 

I actually kind of think I’m good at judging people. I think my taste is pretty good. I feel like I can speak with someone, and more quickly than most, have a sense of where they will fit in and where they won’t; how they can lend their skills, whether they are well placed to help with this grand project of saving the world.

In the best cases, this is symbiotic, maniacal even, me seeing the spark in somebody else and sending it to kindle, them somehow trusting that I saw truly; believing in the me that believes in them. All the worst cases, then, involve having to turn people away. But perhaps there’s another way?

#### The ungated path

Maybe partly in response to the EAG which first turned me away, maybe inspired by Scott Alexander’s [call to open admissions](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/dsCTSCbfHWxmAr2ZT/open-ea-global), I’ve had the policy of never running admissions for Manifest. Anyone is allowed to purchase a ticket and attend. And surprisingly, this seems to have worked okay!

Manifest operates off of positive selection: highlighting specific speakers and topics which show what Manifest should be about, coupled with almost unreasonable amounts of cold outreach and discretionary tickets sent to people who I think would make the experience good. And in the beginning, the nicheness of “prediction markets” ensured that only the nerds would turn out. “Prediction markets” have now exploded, but Manifest has built up a culture of its own. It might help that we don’t advertise too much; that word-of-mouth remains the primary way by which people consider attending each year.

To be determined if this gateless ideal will hold up — the latest incarnation of Manifest pushed Lighthaven to its limits, almost bursting at the seams — but I hope that it will, or that we will be clever enough to find good workarounds. Perhaps we shard attendees throughout days; perhaps we host parts in a park. (In many ways, the free & open-to-public Night Market is the Manifestiest part of Manifest.)

How far can this extend? I recently wrote about how [AI may allow for more porous org boundaries](https://austin.site/posts/fluid-org-boundaries-with-ai). And though AI cometh, we may not need AI for orgs, movements, and states which operate with open borders; just the will to try.

#### Career advice

And, it turns out the world might already be less gated than you think. EA, AI safety, the world overall, are best modeled as doocracies; anyone can “be the change”. If you’re unhappy with how a certain fellowship is run, start your own. If you don’t like how one org hires, create a competitor. My exhortation to the folks who resonate with [aisafety.fail](http://aisafety.fail/) is to carve your own path, do good work in the world, and don’t care overmuch what the gatekeepers think. It’s not the ten (or hundred) rejections that anyone remembers, it’s the one success.

This has been my own experience, anyways.
